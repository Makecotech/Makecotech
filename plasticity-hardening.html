Isotropic, Kinematic or Combined Hardening?

A practical guide to the Bauschinger effect, yield-surface evolution, cyclic plasticity and the choice of hardening model in nonlinear FEA.

Metal Plasticity Nonlinear FEA Cyclic Loading Material Modeling
Plasticity & Hardening

What happens after a metal first yields?

Have you ever wondered what the Bauschinger effect really means, how it is represented in finite element analysis, and why it can matter to an engineering prediction?

Once plastic deformation occurs, a metal does not necessarily respond identically when the loading direction is reversed. The evolution of the yield condition is commonly represented through isotropic hardening, kinematic hardening, or a combination of both.

🧠 Academic Theory: Uniaxial Deviatoric Stress Space Mapping
The deviatoric stress tensor isolates pure shape distortion by removing hydrostatic pressure components, defined as s_ij = σ_ij − ⅓ σ_kk δ_ij . Under a standard pure uniaxial loading state tensor configuration (σ, 0, 0) , the hydrostatic pressure simplifies directly to p = ⅓σ. Subtracting this from the main diagonal yields the principal deviatoric coordinates: s₁ = ⅔σ and s₂ = s₃ = −⅓σ .

Consequently, any standard uniaxial testing path is geometrically constrained to satisfy s₂ = −½ s₁. For J₂ / von Mises plasticity, with s₃ = −(s₁ + s₂), the yield locus in the ordinary s₁–s₂ Cartesian projection is (s₁−a₁)² + (s₁−a₁)(s₂−a₂) + (s₂−a₂)² = σy² / 3 , where a₁ = ⅔α and a₂ = −⅓α. Therefore the J₂ yield locus appears as a rotated ellipse in this s₁–s₂ plot. During active plastic yielding the current stress point lies on this ellipse; during elastic loading/unloading it lies inside it. The configured σsat is retained as the cap for the hardening response.
Isotropic Hardening

Expansion of the yield surface

In isotropic hardening, the center of the yield surface remains fixed while its size increases with accumulated plastic deformation.

σy = σy0 + Hi ε̄p Simplified linear isotropic hardening representation

In a one-dimensional interpretation, further yielding moves farther away from zero stress in both tension and compression. This can be an effective engineering approximation when plastic loading is mainly monotonic and reverse yielding is not important.

Kinematic Hardening

Translation of the yield surface

Kinematic hardening introduces a backstress, α. Instead of simply growing, the yield surface translates in stress space as plastic deformation develops.

dα = Hk dεp Simplified linear kinematic hardening representation

The corresponding one-dimensional yield condition can be written as:

f(σ,α) = |σ − α| − σy ≤ 0

Translation of the yield surface changes the stress required to produce yielding after a reversal in loading direction.

Bauschinger Effect

Why can reverse yielding occur earlier?

Consider a material with an initial yield stress of 200 MPa. After tensile plastic deformation, suppose a positive backstress of 20 MPa has developed.

Forward tensile yield: approximately +220 MPa

Reverse compressive yield: approximately −180 MPa

The material has therefore not simply become stronger in every direction. Its previous plastic deformation has shifted the yield surface. This reduction in reverse yield stress is the essential idea behind the Bauschinger effect.

Engineering Relevance

Why does this matter in FEA?

The hardening formulation becomes especially important when the loading path contains substantial unloading and reverse plasticity.

Application Hardening consideration
Predominantly monotonic plastic loading Isotropic hardening may often be sufficient.
Bending and springback Reverse yielding can influence residual stresses and final geometry.
Bending-unbending over tooling The material experiences repeated changes in plastic-flow direction.
Low-cycle fatigue Cyclic plastic strain and hysteresis behavior depend strongly on the constitutive model.
Thermo-mechanical cycling Repeated thermal expansion and constraint can produce alternating plastic deformation.
Ratcheting Accumulation of plastic strain over repeated cycles requires an appropriate cyclic model.
Combined Hardening

Real materials can both translate and expand

Real metals do not necessarily behave as purely isotropic or purely kinematic systems. Combined hardening allows the yield surface to both translate and change size.

Isotropic contribution

Controls evolution of the yield-surface size with accumulated plastic deformation.

Kinematic contribution

Controls translation of the yield surface and directional memory of previous plastic loading.

Combined response

Provides greater flexibility for representing cyclic stress-strain behavior.

Advanced Constitutive Modeling

Beyond basic hardening laws

The simplified hardening laws above are useful for understanding the core mechanisms, but demanding engineering applications may require more advanced constitutive formulations.

Depending on the material, temperature, strain rate and loading history, the response may involve:

  • Nonlinear isotropic and kinematic hardening
  • Cyclic hardening and cyclic softening
  • Combined nonlinear plasticity formulations
  • Viscoplasticity and strain-rate-dependent behavior
  • Creep and high-temperature time-dependent deformation
  • Damage initiation and damage evolution
  • Ductile and brittle failure
  • Thermo-mechanical constitutive behavior
  • Custom constitutive models and user-material implementations

Model Calibration

The constitutive parameters must represent the loading path

A sophisticated constitutive law is useful only if its parameters are meaningful. A monotonic tensile test may be sufficient for a simple isotropic plasticity model, while cyclic models generally require additional loading-unloading or tension-compression data.

The objective is not to use the most complicated model available, but to select a model that captures the physical mechanisms that materially affect the engineering prediction.

Key Takeaways

The loading path should guide the hardening model

  • Isotropic hardening expands the yield surface.
  • Kinematic hardening translates the yield surface.
  • Kinematic translation provides a mechanism for representing the Bauschinger effect.
  • Combined hardening can represent both translation and expansion.
  • Cyclic applications may require nonlinear hardening formulations.
  • Viscoplasticity, creep, damage or failure may also be important depending on the application.
  • Material parameters should be calibrated against representative experimental data.
