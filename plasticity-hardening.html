Isotropic, Kinematic or Combined Hardening?

A practical guide to how yield surfaces evolve during plastic deformation — and why the choice of hardening model matters for reverse loading, springback, cyclic plasticity and fatigue.

Metal Plasticity Finite Element Analysis Cyclic Loading Material Modeling
Introduction

Why does the hardening model matter?

When a metal plastically deforms, the next question is not only how much it hardens, but how its yield condition evolves.

In finite element analysis, this evolution is commonly represented through isotropic hardening, kinematic hardening, or a combination of both.

For predominantly monotonic loading, different hardening formulations may produce relatively similar results. Once the loading direction changes, however, the selected hardening law can have a significant influence on reverse yielding, residual stress, springback and cyclic plastic response.

1. Isotropic Hardening

The yield surface expands

In isotropic hardening, the center of the yield surface remains fixed while the size of the yield surface increases as plastic deformation accumulates.

σy = σy0 + Hi ε̄p Simplified linear isotropic hardening representation

This means that after plastic deformation, a greater stress magnitude is required to produce further yielding in both loading directions.

Isotropic hardening is therefore often suitable when plastic deformation is predominantly monotonic and the details of load reversal are not critical.

2. Kinematic Hardening

The yield surface translates

In kinematic hardening, the size of the yield surface remains approximately unchanged while its center moves in stress space.

dα = Hk dεp Simplified linear kinematic hardening representation

The displacement of the yield surface is represented by the backstress, α. As plastic deformation develops, the backstress changes and moves the center of the yield locus.

This becomes especially important when the loading direction reverses.

3. Bauschinger Effect

Why reverse yielding can occur earlier

After plastic deformation in one direction, many metals yield at a lower stress magnitude when subsequently loaded in the opposite direction. This phenomenon is known as the Bauschinger effect.

Simple example:

Assume an initial yield stress of 200 MPa. Following tensile plastic deformation, a backstress of +20 MPa develops.

Forward yield ≈ +220 MPa

Reverse yield ≈ −180 MPa

The material has therefore not simply become stronger in every direction. Its previous plastic deformation has changed the location of the yield surface.

Capturing this effect can be important for springback, bending-unbending, low-cycle fatigue and other applications involving substantial load reversal.

4. Combined Hardening

Translation and expansion together

Real metallic materials frequently exhibit both translation and expansion of the yield surface.

Combined isotropic-kinematic hardening allows both mechanisms to act simultaneously.

Translation

Kinematic hardening moves the center of the yield surface and represents directional memory of previous plastic loading.

Expansion

Isotropic hardening changes the size of the yield surface as accumulated plastic deformation increases.

Combined Response

Both mechanisms can be calibrated together to represent more complex cyclic material behavior.

5. Model Selection

When does the difference matter?

The appropriate hardening formulation should be selected based primarily on the expected loading path rather than simply on the material type.

Application Hardening consideration
Predominantly monotonic plastic loading Isotropic hardening may often be sufficient
Bending and springback Kinematic or combined hardening can become important
Bending-unbending over tooling Reverse yielding and the Bauschinger effect should be considered
Low-cycle fatigue Kinematic or combined cyclic plasticity models are generally more relevant
Repeated thermo-mechanical loading Combined hardening may provide a more representative response
Ratcheting and mean-stress evolution More advanced nonlinear cyclic hardening formulations may be required
Practical Takeaway

Choose the model based on the stress path

Isotropic hardening is not automatically inferior to kinematic hardening, and kinematic hardening is not automatically more accurate.

Key points

  • For mostly monotonic plastic deformation, isotropic hardening may provide an adequate engineering representation.
  • When substantial unloading and reverse plasticity occur, translation of the yield surface becomes important.
  • Kinematic hardening provides a mechanism for representing the Bauschinger effect.
  • Combined hardening can represent both expansion and translation.
  • Constitutive parameters should ideally be calibrated using experimental data representative of the actual loading path and plastic strain range.
