Isotropic, Kinematic or Combined Hardening?

Understanding how the yield surface evolves is essential when plastic deformation involves unloading, load reversal or cyclic loading.

Metal Plasticity Nonlinear FEA Cyclic Loading Material Modeling
Plasticity & Hardening

What happens after a metal first yields?

Have you ever questioned what the Bauschinger effect actually means, how it is represented in a finite element material model, and why it can matter to an engineering prediction?

Once plastic deformation occurs, a metal does not necessarily respond identically when the loading direction is reversed. The way the yield condition evolves is commonly represented through isotropic hardening, kinematic hardening, or a combination of both.

Isotropic Hardening

Expansion of the yield surface

In isotropic hardening, the center of the yield surface remains fixed while its size increases as plastic deformation accumulates.

σy = σy0 + Hi ε̄p Simplified linear isotropic hardening formulation

In a one-dimensional interpretation, yielding therefore moves farther away from zero stress in both tension and compression.

This behavior is often adequate when the plastic loading is mainly monotonic and reverse yielding is not an important part of the response.

Kinematic Hardening

Translation of the yield surface

Kinematic hardening introduces a backstress, usually denoted by α. Instead of simply expanding, the yield surface translates in stress space.

dα = Hk dεp Simplified linear kinematic hardening representation

The corresponding one-dimensional yield condition can be written as:

f(σ,α) = |σ − α| − σy ≤ 0

This translation creates an important asymmetry after prior plastic deformation: yielding can occur earlier when the loading direction reverses.

Bauschinger Effect

Why does reverse yielding occur earlier?

Consider a material with an initial yield stress of 200 MPa.

After plastic loading in tension, suppose a positive backstress of 20 MPa has developed. The tensile and compressive yield limits become approximately:

Forward tensile yield: +220 MPa

Reverse compressive yield: −180 MPa

The material has therefore not simply become stronger. Its previous plastic deformation has changed the position of the yield surface.

This reduction in reverse yield stress is the basic mechanical idea behind the Bauschinger effect.

Engineering Relevance

Why does this matter in FEA?

The choice of hardening model becomes particularly important whenever the loading path includes significant unloading and reverse plasticity.

Application Why hardening matters
Predominantly monotonic loading Isotropic hardening may often provide an adequate engineering representation.
Bending and springback Reverse yielding can influence residual stress and final springback.
Bending-unbending over tooling The material can experience repeated changes in the direction of plastic flow.
Low-cycle fatigue The hysteresis loop and cyclic plastic strain depend strongly on the cyclic plasticity model.
Thermo-mechanical cycling Repeated thermal expansion and constraint can generate alternating plastic deformation.
Ratcheting Accumulation of plastic strain over repeated cycles requires appropriate cyclic hardening behavior.
Combined Hardening

Real materials can both translate and expand

Real metallic materials do not necessarily behave as purely isotropic or purely kinematic systems.

Combined hardening allows the yield surface to both translate and change size.

Isotropic contribution

Controls the evolution of the yield-surface size with accumulated plastic deformation.

Kinematic contribution

Controls translation of the yield surface and directional memory of previous plastic deformation.

Combined response

Allows a more flexible representation of cyclic stress-strain behavior.

Advanced Constitutive Modeling

The real material response may be considerably more complex

The simplified hardening laws above are useful for understanding the physical mechanisms, but advanced engineering simulations may require substantially richer constitutive behavior.

Depending on the material, temperature, strain rate and loading history, the response may include:

  • Nonlinear isotropic and kinematic hardening
  • Cyclic hardening and cyclic softening
  • Combined nonlinear plasticity formulations
  • Viscoplasticity and strain-rate-dependent behavior
  • Creep and high-temperature time-dependent deformation
  • Damage initiation and damage evolution
  • Ductile failure and brittle failure
  • Thermo-mechanical constitutive behavior
  • Custom constitutive formulations and user-material implementations

Model Calibration

A sophisticated model is only useful if its parameters are meaningful

Hardening parameters should ideally be calibrated using experimental data representative of the actual loading path and strain range.

A monotonic tensile test may be sufficient for a simple isotropic plasticity model, but cyclic material models generally require additional loading-unloading or tension-compression data.

The objective is not to use the most complicated model available, but to use a constitutive description that captures the mechanisms that materially influence the engineering prediction.

Key Takeaways

The loading path should guide the hardening model

  • Isotropic hardening expands the yield surface.
  • Kinematic hardening translates the yield surface.
  • Translation allows reverse yielding and the Bauschinger effect to be represented.
  • Combined hardening can capture both translation and expansion.
  • For cyclic plasticity, more advanced nonlinear hardening laws may be required.
  • Viscoplasticity, creep, damage and failure may also need to be included depending on the application.
  • Material parameters should be calibrated against representative experimental data.
