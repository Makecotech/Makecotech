Isotropic, Kinematic or Combined Hardening?

A practical guide to understanding how different hardening models influence yielding, reverse loading, cyclic plasticity and finite element predictions.

Metal Plasticity

Why does the hardening model matter?

When a metal plastically deforms, its subsequent response depends not only on the amount of plastic strain, but also on how the yield condition evolves.

In finite element analysis, this evolution is commonly represented using isotropic hardening, kinematic hardening, or a combination of both.

For predominantly monotonic loading, different hardening formulations may produce relatively similar results. However, when the loading direction changes, the selected hardening law can strongly influence reverse yielding, residual stresses, springback and cyclic plastic response.

1. Isotropic Hardening

The yield surface expands

In isotropic hardening, the center of the yield surface remains fixed while the size of the yield surface increases as plastic deformation accumulates.

σy = σy0 + Hi ε̄p

This means that after plastic deformation in one direction, a larger stress magnitude is required to cause yielding in either direction.

Isotropic hardening can therefore be effective when the material response is dominated by monotonic plastic deformation and the details of load reversal are not important.

2. Kinematic Hardening

The yield surface translates

In kinematic hardening, the size of the yield surface remains approximately unchanged while its center moves in stress space.

dα = Hk dεp

The translation is represented by the backstress, α. This shift changes the stress required to produce yielding when the loading direction reverses.

This behavior is particularly important when modeling repeated loading, unloading and reverse plastic deformation.

3. The Bauschinger Effect

Why reverse yielding can occur earlier

After plastic deformation in one direction, many metals yield at a lower stress magnitude when subsequently loaded in the opposite direction. This phenomenon is known as the Bauschinger effect.

Consider a material with an initial yield stress of 200 MPa. After tensile plastic deformation, the backstress may shift the center of the yield surface by +20 MPa.

Forward yield: +220 MPa
Reverse yield: −180 MPa

The material has not simply become stronger in all directions. Instead, its previous plastic deformation has changed the position of the yield surface.

4. Combined Hardening

Translation and expansion together

Real metallic materials often exhibit both translation and expansion of the yield surface.

Combined isotropic-kinematic hardening allows both mechanisms to act simultaneously:

  • Kinematic hardening controls translation of the yield surface.
  • Isotropic hardening controls expansion of the yield surface.
  • The combined formulation can represent more realistic cyclic response.

5. When Does It Matter?

Loading path is the key

The appropriate hardening model should be selected based on the expected loading path rather than simply on the material type.

Application Hardening consideration
Predominantly monotonic plastic loading Isotropic hardening may often be sufficient
Bending and springback Kinematic or combined hardening can become important
Bending-unbending over tooling Reverse yielding and Bauschinger effect should be considered
Low-cycle fatigue Kinematic or combined cyclic plasticity models are generally more relevant
Repeated thermo-mechanical loading Combined hardening may be required
Ratcheting and mean-stress evolution More advanced nonlinear cyclic hardening models may be necessary

Practical Takeaway

Choose the model based on the stress path

Isotropic hardening is not automatically inferior to kinematic hardening, and kinematic hardening is not automatically more accurate.

The important question is whether the application involves significant unloading, reverse plasticity or cyclic deformation.

For monotonic loading, a simple isotropic model may be fully adequate. For cyclic or reversing loading paths, capturing translation of the yield surface and the Bauschinger effect can become essential.

As always, the constitutive model should be calibrated against experimental data representative of the intended loading path and strain range.
